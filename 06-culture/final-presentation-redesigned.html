Module 1Evaluation strategy
User promise

For VP-level strategists and product leaders at Fortune 500 companies, Ascend IQ promises to answer plain-language market questions in seconds with facts that match our verified sources, so that they can act on those answers in high-stakes decisions and keep renewing their $50k+ subscriptions.

Three trust metrics
  • Latency
  • Hallucination Rate
  • Robustness

Why: They cover the three parts of the promise. Hallucination Rate covers verified, Robustness covers plain-language questions, and Latency covers in seconds. Fairness and UX Trust matter less for this launch, which is 50 accounts asking factual questions.

Trade-offs
  • Hallucination Rate ↔ Latency: We prioritize Hallucination Rate over Latency because a slower verified answer costs a VP a few seconds, while a fast wrong one in a board deck can cost us a $50k account.
  • Hallucination Rate ↔ Robustness: We prioritize Hallucination Rate over Robustness because saying “I can’t verify that” on an edge-case question is recoverable, while a confident fabrication is not. We’ll limit refusals to questions the data truly can’t answer.

Periodic audit · Context Specificity (Robustness · P1): On every answer, an automated check compares the filters in the question (region, date range, sentiment, source) with the filters actually applied in retrieval and blocks any answer that dropped one. Each week, a reviewer also samples 50 multi-part and filtered answers to catch subtler misses the automated check can't see., enough because The automated filter check catches the dangerous case, an answer that quietly ignored "APAC" or "last 30 days", on every answer, not just in a sample; the weekly audit catches partial misses in meaning. What it misses is an answer that applied the right filters but still focused on the wrong part of the question, and we accept that until the audit shows it recurring, at which point we buy this an online eval next quarter.

Periodic audit · Data Bias (Fairness · P2): Each week, a reviewer samples 50 answers per region (US, EMEA, APAC) and scores coverage, data quality and sentiment-scoring consistency against the sources, comparing each region's scores with the US baseline., enough because Bias is a pattern that shows up across many answers, which a per-region weekly sample is built to measure. It misses single skewed answers between audits; we move it to an online eval if any region's quality score falls more than 10 points below the US baseline in two consecutive audits.

Insights

What I learned

Where the course was hardest, what you’ll keep doing, and the moment your thinking changed.

Friction

Trusting the tools. My first eval run came back inverted because my rule check's pass and fail conditions said the same thing, and an AI "CPO review" of my budget recommended a swap that broke the $200K cap because it priced an online eval at $25K instead of $70K. I learned to check every number an LLM gives me, and to write checks so two people would score them the same way.

Key learnings

Start from real failures, not generic metrics. Use cheap deterministic rules for what you can define exactly and an LLM judge only for meaning. Set thresholds from measured catch and false-alarm counts. Gate each dimension on its own instead of one blended score. Pick a gate from business risk, not from the failure label. And accept a gap only with a named kill criterion.

Aha moment

When my judge's two "false alarms" turned out to be real errors my own audit had missed. I had treated my human labels as ground truth, but neither the human nor the judge is ground truth on its own: you compare them and investigate where they disagree.
